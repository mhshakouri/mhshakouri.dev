## The project that broke the assumption

{/* Arrowword: two people solving a photographed Persian crossword together
    on separate devices. Started as a demo. By the time the spec was done it
    had a Durable Object per session, R2 storage, a WebSocket protocol, and
    six milestones.

    The tell: it had users. Not visitors, users. You and your partner, with
    data you would be annoyed to lose. */}

## Demo versus product

{/* The distinction worth naming, because it is the whole decision:

    A demo is finishable in a few evenings, has no backend, no data worth
    keeping, and no users. It belongs on your site.

    A product has its own release cadence, its own dependencies, and someone
    who notices when it breaks. It does not.

    Your own test: does it have a backend, and would anyone care if it went
    down? */}

## What actually goes wrong when you mix them

{/* Four costs, all real, roughly in order of how much they bite:
    - dependency pollution: a demo's image library widens the site's install
      time and security surface
    - a broken experiment blocks a blog post, because push to main deploys
      the site
    - two lifecycles in one git history, one stable and one churning
    - a reader browsing the repo sees a website plus half-built apps

    Worth being honest that none of these had bitten yet when you split. You
    moved on principle, and the principle is that the cheap moment to split
    is before the coupling exists, not after. */}

## What I do now

{/* Own repo, own deploy, own subdomain. arrowword.mhshakouri.dev. The site
    links out and describes the work; it never hosts it.

    The cost you accepted: design tokens get copied rather than imported, and
    every project carries its own CI. Say whether that has hurt yet. */}
